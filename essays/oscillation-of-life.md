# The Oscillation of Life

*On Reality, Intelligence, Growth, and Gratitude*

I believe intelligent life exists in an endless oscillation between two activities: **growth and gratitude**.

Growth is the expansion of what we can consider and experience. Gratitude is the appreciation of what we have come to find beautiful through experience.

One expands our experience of reality. The other appreciates that experience.

This is more than a philosophy for living. I believe it follows from the very nature of reality.

My conjecture begins with a simple claim: **nothing exists in isolation.**

## Nothing Exists in Isolation

Consider a pencil.

We can describe its length, weight, color, or the materials from which it was made. Each description tells us something about the pencil, but none tells us everything.

We can look closer.

The pencil consists of atoms, which interact through physical forces. Its wood came from a tree, which grew through sunlight, water, and nutrients from the soil. Its graphite has a geological history. The pencil itself was manufactured by people, transported through an economy, and eventually placed in someone's hand.

We could investigate the history of the people who made it, the evolution of the trees from which it came, or the physical processes that gave rise to its materials.

We could also investigate its relationship to the person holding it. What does the pencil mean to them? What will they write? What thoughts might it inspire?

Every answer opens new questions.

Where does the pencil end?

We might draw a boundary around its physical surface, but doing so excludes its interactions with the environment. We could expand the boundary to include those interactions, but this would introduce further interactions beyond the new boundary.

There is no reason the process must stop.

The important point is not that we have failed to discover the pencil's ultimate boundary. It is that **the boundary itself is something we introduce when we describe the pencil.**

To identify a pencil, we must distinguish it from everything else. We choose what belongs to the pencil and what does not.

That distinction is useful, but it is arbitrary in a fundamental sense. We could draw it differently.

We might define the pencil by its physical surface, its chemical composition, its history, or its function. Each definition serves a different purpose. None establishes that the pencil exists independently of everything else.

The same is true of every object, event, and process we can consider.

Even relationships require us to distinguish the things being related. If we say that a pencil is connected to a desk, we have already distinguished the pencil, the desk, and their relationship.

Those distinctions are representations.

By *representation*, I mean any way of describing or making sense of reality. A word, a measurement, a scientific theory, or a mental image is a representation.

Representations allow us to think about reality by selecting certain features while leaving others aside.

But a representation is not reality itself.

Every representation can be expanded. Every boundary we draw can be reconsidered. Every distinction introduces further possibilities for investigation.

This leads me to a bold conjecture:

**Reality has no fundamental boundaries or distinctions.**

In philosophy, a claim about what fundamentally exists is called an *ontological* claim. My ontological conjecture is that reality is an indivisible whole.

The objects and relationships we encounter are useful ways of distinguishing parts of that whole. But there is no fundamental separation between them.

Nothing exists in isolation.

## Reality as an Unbounded Graph

How can we imagine a reality without boundaries or distinctions?

The closest mathematical model I have found is an **uncountably infinite complete graph**.

A graph is simply a collection of points, called nodes, and connections between them, called edges.

A complete graph is one in which every node connects to every other node.

Imagine that every distinction we could possibly make about reality becomes a node. A pencil, a person, a thought, an emotion, a physical interaction, or a scientific theory could each be represented by a node.

Every node would connect to every other node.

But we could also create a node representing the relationship between two other nodes. We could represent the relationship between those relationships, and then represent those new relationships.

The process continues.

The graph would need to include representations of itself, because those representations are also part of reality.

And there would always be more to represent.

I use *uncountably infinite* to describe my conjecture that the possible distinctions of reality are not merely an endless list, but exceed what could be captured by such a list. In mathematics, uncountable infinity is a larger kind of infinity than the infinity of whole numbers.

An endless process alone does not prove that particular mathematical size. The graph is a way to express my conjecture, not a proof of its exact size.

More importantly, **reality does not literally consist of nodes and edges**.

The graph is another representation. Its nodes and edges are distinctions we introduce to make reality easier to consider.

Reality itself has no such fundamental divisions.

This also means that the representation and the person constructing it belong to the same reality being represented.

A thought about reality is part of reality. A mathematical proof is part of reality. A computer simulation is part of reality.

There is no place outside reality from which to observe it.

Consider the stock market.

Suppose someone discovers a reliable way to predict future stock prices. If enough people act on that prediction, their actions change the market. The prediction itself becomes part of the system it was intended to predict.

Our attempts to understand reality are not separate from reality. They are activities occurring within it.

Every representation becomes another thing we can consider.

We are pulling on an endless string.

The more we investigate, the more there is to investigate.

**Reality is boundless. Our representations never exhaust it.**

## The Frame Problem

This leads to a problem that I believe every intelligence must face.

It is called the *frame problem*.

In simple terms, the frame problem concerns deciding what matters when we try to understand or solve something.

Imagine trying to predict where a pencil will land when dropped.

We might need to consider gravity, the pencil's shape, its starting position, and the surface below it.

But should we also consider air currents? The temperature of the room? The movement of people nearby? The gravitational effects of the moon?

We could keep adding details.

Most of them would make little difference to our prediction. But how do we know which ones matter?

To solve the problem, we must establish a *frame*: a boundary around what we will consider.

Every model does this.

A model is a representation designed to help us understand, explain, predict, or act.

When we construct a model, we select certain features of reality and exclude others. We simplify the world so we can reason about it.

That simplification is necessary.

We cannot include every possible distinction in every explanation. Even the most advanced intelligence must decide what to consider.

But if reality has no fundamental boundaries, then neither does the problem we are modeling.

Every frame is one we have constructed. The reality outside it remains connected to the reality within it.

A detail we excluded might turn out to matter.

And if we expand the frame to include it, we introduce further details that might also matter.

**The frame problem can never be eliminated because every representation necessarily establishes a frame within a boundless reality.**

Greater intelligence can create better models. It can discover which details are important, replace poor assumptions, and develop more powerful explanations.

But no amount of intelligence creates a fundamental boundary where none exists.

This is why I find the philosophy of Karl Popper and David Deutsch so compelling.

They argue that knowledge grows through conjecture and criticism.

A *conjecture* is a proposed explanation. We create explanations, look for their flaws, and improve or replace them.

We do not need certainty to make progress.

We need explanations that survive criticism and help us understand more of reality.

Sometimes this requires detailed scientific models. Sometimes it requires a simple estimate.

For example, a Fermi estimate breaks a complicated question into a small number of quantities that we can reasonably approximate.

We don't need to understand every detail of the world's economy to estimate the number of piano tuners in a city. We need a few useful assumptions.

A rough model can be more useful than an elaborate one if it preserves the relationships that matter.

The challenge is choosing the right representation.

This also explains why tools can both help and hinder understanding.

A mathematical formula, a programming language, or a scientific instrument makes certain problems easier to solve. But each tool also limits what we can readily express or investigate.

A tool can make us exceptionally good at searching within a particular representation while preventing us from considering a better representation.

Intelligence must therefore improve not only its answers, but also the ways it searches for answers.

The frame problem is not a temporary technical difficulty. It is a permanent feature of representing reality.

And this gives intelligence an endless opportunity.

There is always another frame to expand, another assumption to question, and another explanation to discover.

## Intelligence and Growth

What is intelligence?

I propose that intelligence is part of reality's ongoing process of creating and improving representations.

Intelligence distinguishes, explains, predicts, experiments, and learns.

It constructs models of reality, discovers their limitations, and develops better ones.

The process can even turn upon itself.

We can think about our own thinking. We can investigate how we learn, how we reason, and how our tools influence what we understand.

This is sometimes called *metareasoning*: reasoning about how we reason.

But it is still modeling. We are simply using representations to improve the process of representation itself.

The deeper point is that intelligence does not stand outside reality and examine it.

Intelligence is an activity within reality.

As it develops, it expands what can be considered.

A child learns to distinguish colors, shapes, and sounds. Later, they may learn mathematics, music, science, and philosophy. Each new understanding gives them access to relationships they could not previously recognize.

The same pattern appears in scientific discovery.

Understanding gravity gave us new ways to explain the movement of planets. Understanding evolution revealed relationships among living organisms. Understanding computation opened new possibilities for machines and intelligence.

Each discovery changes what we can consider and what we can do.

This is what I mean by **growth**.

Growth is the expansion of what is being considered, as an experience.

It is not limited to acquiring facts or improving physical abilities.

We grow when we learn, experiment, create, build, travel, develop relationships, encounter unfamiliar ideas, or discover new ways of experiencing the world.

Growth expands our experience of reality.

And because reality is boundless, there is no final point at which growth must be complete.

There is always more to discover.

But growth alone leaves an important question unanswered.

**Why should we care about discovering more?**

## Beauty and Gratitude

We often explain intelligence through its practical benefits.

Intelligence helps us survive, predict danger, obtain resources, and solve problems.

These abilities matter. Biological evolution gives us good reasons why organisms with such abilities would succeed.

But I do not think survival or practical usefulness captures the full value of intelligence.

I believe intelligence has another remarkable capacity: **the ability to appreciate beauty**.

Consider the experience of discovering an elegant explanation.

Something that once seemed complicated suddenly becomes simple. Events that appeared unrelated become understandable through a common mechanism.

There is beauty in that discovery.

There is also beauty in a piece of music, a mountain landscape, a mathematical proof, a loving relationship, a moment of physical movement, or a conversation that changes how we see the world.

These experiences differ, yet we can appreciate each of them for its own sake.

Beauty is difficult to define.

It is not simply symmetry, simplicity, or pleasure. We can find beauty in complexity, uncertainty, struggle, and mystery.

I don't yet have a complete explanation for why intelligence values beauty.

But I have a conjecture.

**Intelligence expands the possibilities of experience, and those experiences expand the possibilities of appreciation.**

The more we understand, the more we may find beautiful.

A person who studies music can hear relationships they once missed. A person who studies astronomy can look at the night sky with a richer understanding of what they are seeing. A person who learns about another human being can discover new depths of appreciation and love.

Understanding does not merely provide information.

It can transform experience.

And the remarkable thing is that discovering more does not necessarily make reality less mysterious.

An explanation can reveal an entire new landscape of questions.

There is beauty in what we understand, and beauty in what remains to be discovered.

This reminds me of the religious idea of *glory*.

In the Christian tradition, the beauty and majesty of creation are sometimes described as evidence of the divine.

I do not believe we need a supernatural creator to appreciate that beauty. Nor do I accept the narrow ethical claims often attached to those traditions.

But I recognize the experience they are trying to describe.

Reality is extraordinary.

The emergence of stars, planets, life, minds, knowledge, relationships, and experience is worthy of profound appreciation.

We need not imagine an intelligence outside reality admiring what it has created.

Intelligence itself is part of reality.

**Perhaps intelligence is one of the ways reality comes to appreciate itself.**

This is where gratitude enters my philosophy.

Gratitude is the appreciation of what we have come to find beautiful, as an experience.

It is the act of recognizing the value of what we have encountered.

A discovery, a friendship, a relationship, a beautiful idea, a moment of joy, or simply the opportunity to experience existence can all become sources of gratitude.

Growth expands what we encounter.

Gratitude appreciates what we encounter.

And each can inspire the other.

## The Oscillation of Life

This brings us to the central idea.

**Intelligent life exists in an endless oscillation of growth and gratitude.**

Growth expands the possibilities of experience.

Gratitude appreciates the beauty found within those experiences.

Growth without gratitude becomes an endless pursuit of the next thing.

We work toward the next achievement, the next discovery, the next improvement, or the next experience, never appreciating what we have already found.

Life becomes something that will be worthwhile later.

Gratitude without growth has the opposite limitation.

We appreciate what we already know while neglecting the possibilities that remain unexplored.

We risk confusing contentment with the end of discovery.

Growth and gratitude belong together.

We explore and discover. We appreciate what we have encountered. Our appreciation inspires further exploration, which reveals more possibilities for appreciation.

This is not a strict alternation between two separate states.

Growth and gratitude often happen simultaneously.

A scientist can appreciate the beauty of a discovery while exploring its implications. A parent can delight in a child as they are while helping them grow. A person can enjoy movement while developing greater strength and skill.

Sometimes the most meaningful experiences are those in which growth and gratitude become indistinguishable.

Nor does gratitude require us to appreciate suffering or approve of everything that happens.

We can love life while working to reduce suffering, prevent destruction, and improve the circumstances of ourselves and others.

The oscillation is not a command to remain satisfied with reality as we currently experience it.

It is an invitation to participate in it.

To grow is to expand what can be experienced.

To be grateful is to appreciate what has become beautiful through experience.

One makes the other richer.

And because reality is boundless, there is no final destination.

## Heads, Hands, and Hearts

I find it useful to organize intelligent life into three broad categories: **heads, hands, and hearts**.

### Heads — Cognition

Heads represent our capacity to understand.

We reason, learn, explain, imagine, and discover.

Cognition expands what we can consider. It allows us to recognize relationships and possibilities that were previously unavailable to us.

### Hands — Labor

Hands represent our capacity to transform.

We build, create, experiment, move, cultivate, and change our circumstances.

Labor turns possibilities into experiences. It allows us to act upon our understanding and reshape the world around us.

### Hearts — Social and Experiential Life

Hearts represent our capacity to experience and appreciate.

We love, connect, play, form communities, enjoy beauty, and find meaning in our relationships and experiences.

Hearts remind us why understanding and transformation matter.

These are not three separate parts of reality. They are useful distinctions within the same activity of living.

Understanding allows us to imagine new possibilities. Labor makes many of those possibilities real. Experience allows us to appreciate them.

And the relationships work in every direction.

Love inspires learning. Work leads to discovery. Understanding deepens relationships. Gratitude inspires creation. New experiences change what we find beautiful.

Heads, hands, and hearts are complementary expressions of growth and gratitude.

This framework also shapes how I think about technological progress.

Artificial intelligence and automation are rapidly expanding our ability to understand and transform reality.

Much of the cognitive and physical labor that once required human effort may increasingly be performed by machines.

I see enormous promise in this development.

If technology reduces the labor required to meet our needs, it can expand the time and resources available for social and experiential life.

Human beings may gain extraordinary opportunities for community, relationships, play, discovery, physical activity, creativity, and experiences we can scarcely imagine today.

Technological progress need not diminish the value of human life by reducing the amount of work we must perform.

It can expand the possibilities of living.

Heads and hands create possibilities.

Hearts help us appreciate them.

Even in a world of extraordinary abundance, we will still have reasons to grow, discover, love, and experience.

And if reality is boundless, those possibilities will never be exhausted.

## An Endless Oscillation

I began with the claim that nothing exists in isolation.

Every object, event, property, and relationship we distinguish is part of an indivisible reality.

Every boundary is representational. Every representation can be expanded. No distinction we draw establishes a fundamental separation from the whole.

The best explanation is that **reality itself is boundless and without fundamental distinctions**.

Intelligence is an activity within that reality, constructing and improving representations through which more can be understood and experienced.

The frame problem ensures that this process never reaches a final, complete representation.

There is always more to consider.

And as intelligence expands what can be considered, it expands the possibilities of experience and appreciation.

This gives rise to my philosophy for living.

**Growth is the expansion of what I can consider and experience.**

**Gratitude is the appreciation of what I have come to find beautiful through experience.**

These activities reinforce one another.

Discovery reveals beauty. Beauty inspires discovery.

Life need not be a journey toward some final state of completion. There is no need to finish reality, master every possibility, or reach a point beyond which nothing remains worth exploring.

The possibilities are endless.

We can continue to discover, create, understand, experience, and appreciate.

And we can recognize the beauty of doing so while the process unfolds.

**To discover more of reality, and to love more of what we discover.**
