# Growth and Gratitude

## Introduction

What is intelligence for? The usual answer is survival. Intelligence helps us find food, avoid danger, and solve problems. That answer is true, but I don't think it is complete.

My conjecture is that intelligent life moves back and forth between two activities: **growth** and **gratitude**. Growth is expanding what we can consider and experience. Gratitude is appreciating what we have come to find beautiful. The two work like a swing, each motion feeding the next: growth gives us more to appreciate, and appreciation draws us to grow.

This is a philosophy for living, but it rests on a view of reality and of minds. So the essay has three parts. **Reality** argues that reality has no edges. **Minds** explains how minds divide it into things anyway, and why no mind can ever see all of it. **Living** draws the consequence: growth never has to end, and there is always more to appreciate. The first two parts are what philosophers call ontology, the study of what exists, and epistemology, the study of how we know.

## Reality

### 1. Nothing Exists in Isolation

Consider a pencil. We can describe its length, weight, and color. Each description tells us something true about it, and none tells us everything.

Look closer and the pencil keeps opening up. It is made of atoms held together by physical forces. Its wood came from a tree that grew from sunlight, water, and soil. Its graphite formed deep in the earth over millions of years. People mined, cut, and assembled it, and an economy carried it to the hand that holds it. Leonard Read made this point in his essay "I, Pencil": no single person on earth knows how to make one. And the pencil has a future. What will its owner write? What thoughts will it help them have?

Every answer opens new questions. So where does the pencil end?

We could draw a line at its surface. But that leaves out everything it touches and everything that touches it. We could widen the line to include those interactions, but then there are new interactions just past the new line. Nothing forces the widening to stop.

The point isn't that we haven't found the pencil's true edge yet. It is that reality has no edges to find. Everything is related to everything else, and nothing is separated.

This leads to a bold conjecture: **reality has no edges or distinctions of its own.** The edges we see are lines drawn by observers, not walls built into the world. Who draws them, and how, is the subject of the rest of this part and the next.

### 2. Reality as an Endless Graph

How can we picture a reality with no edges? The closest picture I know comes from mathematics.

A **graph**, in math, is a set of points, called *nodes*, joined by lines. Mathematicians call these lines edges, but I'll call them *links*, to keep them apart from the edges this essay is about. A subway map is a graph: the stations are nodes, and the tracks between them are links. A **complete graph** is one where every node links to every other node.

Now imagine that every distinction we could ever make about reality becomes a node: a pencil, a person, a thought, a feeling, a theory. Every node links to every other. But the links are things we can think about too, so each one can become a node of its own. Then the links between those links can become nodes, and so on without end. The graph would even have to include pictures of itself, since those are part of reality too.

How big would this graph be? Some infinities are bigger than others. The whole numbers (1, 2, 3, and so on) never end, but you can list them in order, and every number shows up somewhere on the list. In the 1870s the mathematician Georg Cantor proved that the points on a line can't be listed that way: any list you make will miss some. Mathematicians call an infinity you can list *countable*, and one you can't *uncountable*. I conjecture that the distinctions we could make about reality are uncountable, like the points on a line: not just endless, but too many to ever list.

Reality is not literally made of nodes and links. The graph is just a picture, a way of making a boundless reality easier to think about.

### 3. Observers

If reality has no edges, where does the pencil's edge come from? It comes from an **observer**: a part of reality that takes in information about the rest and separates it into things.

The graph makes one fact about observers vivid. The person drawing it is inside it. A thought about reality is part of reality. So is a mathematical proof, a computer simulation, or this essay. There is no place outside reality to stand and look at it. An observer is not separate from what it observes.

So our attempts to understand reality aren't separate from it either. Each one is a new part of reality, related to everything else, and so each one adds something new to understand. It is like pulling on an endless string: the more we pull, the more there is to pull.

Carl Sagan said that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself. That is the picture here. Reality has no edges of its own, but it contains observers, and through them it shows up as pencils, people, and stars. How an observer turns an edgeless reality into things depends on what kind of observer it is.

## Minds

### 4. Minds and Their Capabilities

The only observers we know of are minds: biological and now digital. So from here on I'll speak of minds.

A mind takes in reality through what it can do. I'll call these its **capabilities**: its sensors, its memory, its intelligence, and the substrate it runs on, whether neurons or silicon. Capabilities set what a mind can take in. A bat hears the shapes of things in the dark. A microscope shows us what our eyes can't. A mind with more memory can hold more of what it takes in at once.

Whatever its capabilities, no mind can take in all of reality. Every mind we know of is finite, and reality has no edges. So a mind does something else: it makes copies.

### 5. Lossy Representations

Everything a mind perceives is a **representation**: a lossy copy of edgeless reality, made of information. *Lossy* means it keeps some of reality and leaves the rest out. A word is a representation. So is a measurement, a map, a scientific theory, or a picture in your mind. The loss is what makes a representation useful, and it is why a representation is never the thing itself. Even a relationship is a representation. To say the pencil is *on* the desk, we first have to separate the pencil, the desk, and the "on."

A representation separates in two ways. The first is **resolution**: the scale at which it shows things as single units. Resolution is a feature of the mind, not of reality, and the mind's capabilities shape it. A human eye gives one resolution, a microscope another, a telescope another.

Change the resolution and different things appear. At the resolution of atoms, there is no pencil. If we widened our view but kept that resolution, we would see a vast arrangement of atoms, with no line where the pencil stops and the hand holding it begins. At the resolution of a person looking at a desk, the pencil is plain to see, and no atom is. Look at scales smaller than atoms and matter stops looking like objects at all. It looks like fields, waves, and energy. Look at larger scales and new units appear: planets, stars, galaxies. Nothing says this has to stop at our universe. If we could see many universes interacting, our universe might look the way a pencil looks to us: one thing among others, part of something larger.

At every scale, new things emerge that can't be seen at the others. Small isn't more real than large. No resolution is the true one, and none shows reality as it is apart from a mind, because every resolution belongs to one.

The second is **perspective**: what the mind includes and what it leaves out. Even at one resolution, we choose where to separate. To talk about a pencil at all, we have to separate it from everything else. We could define it by its surface, its chemistry, its history, or its use. Each choice serves a purpose. None shows that the pencil exists apart from everything else.

So the pencil's edge belongs to a mind with a particular resolution and perspective, not to reality. Some edges seem to come from nature, not from us. A cell has a membrane. A planet has a surface. These are good places to draw lines, because at our usual resolution things change sharply there. But change the resolution and the sharpness goes away. At the resolution of molecules, a membrane is a crowd of molecules trading with the outside all the time. What looked like a wall is a place where a mind, at one resolution and from one perspective, finds it useful to draw a line.

Every edge we have ever drawn can be redrawn, and no investigation has ever reached a final one. The conjecture explains why: every investigation, followed far enough, opens onto more.

### 6. The Frame Problem

If reality has no edges, every mind faces a problem that can never be fully solved. It is called the **frame problem**: how do we decide what matters when we try to understand something? It began as a technical puzzle in early artificial intelligence research, and philosophers soon saw that it applies to every mind. I wrote about it at length in [More Better](frame-problem.md).

Imagine predicting where a pencil will land when you drop it. You need gravity, the pencil's shape, where you dropped it from, and the surface below. But what about air currents? The temperature of the room? People walking by? The pull of the moon? You could keep adding details forever. Most would make almost no difference, but how do you know which ones?

To answer at all, you have to set a **frame**: a boundary around what you will consider. A frame is a perspective applied to reality, where everything is related to everything else. Every model does this. A **model** is a representation built to help us understand, explain, predict, or act. Building one means keeping some features of the world and leaving out the rest. A model also picks a resolution. To predict the pencil's fall, we treat it as one solid object, not a cloud of atoms, because at that resolution the atoms don't change the answer. That isn't a flaw. No mind, however powerful, can include everything in every explanation.

But if reality has no edges, neither does any problem we try to model. Every frame is one we drew, and the world outside it stays connected to the world inside. A detail we left out might turn out to matter. If we widen the frame to include it, new details appear just past the new edge, and they might matter too. So the frame problem can't be eliminated. Every representation draws a frame, and reality never fits inside one.

Greater intelligence helps. It builds better models, finds which details matter, and replaces bad assumptions. But no amount of intelligence can create a final edge where reality has none.

This is why I find the philosophy of Karl Popper and David Deutsch so useful here. They argue that knowledge grows through conjecture and criticism. A *conjecture* is a proposed explanation. We propose one, look for its flaws, and improve it or replace it. We don't need certainty to make progress. We need explanations that hold up under criticism and help us understand more.

Sometimes that takes a detailed scientific model. Sometimes a rough estimate is better. The physicist Enrico Fermi was famous for asking students questions like "How many piano tuners are there in Chicago?" No one knows the answer offhand, but you can estimate it from a few guesses: how many people live there, how many homes have pianos, how often a piano needs tuning, and how many pianos one tuner can handle in a year. A simple model that keeps the relationships that matter can beat a complicated one that buries them. The skill is choosing the right frame.

Tools work the same way. Algebra, a programming language, or a microscope makes some problems easy and others hard to even see. A tool can make us very good at searching inside one way of looking at a problem while keeping us from finding a better way. So intelligence has to improve not only its answers but also how it looks for them.

The frame problem is not a temporary hurdle. It is a permanent feature of being a mind in a boundless world. That sounds like bad news, but it is also an endless opportunity. There is always another frame to widen, another assumption to question, and another explanation to find.

### 7. Intelligence and Growth

What, then, is intelligence? My proposal is that intelligence is the capability a mind uses to build and improve its representations. It notices differences, explains, predicts, experiments, and learns. It builds models, finds where they fail, and builds better ones.

It can even turn on itself. We can think about our own thinking: how we learn, how we reason, and how our tools shape what we understand. But this is still modeling. We are using representations to improve the way we make representations. And it still happens inside reality, not above it.

As intelligence develops, it widens what can be considered. A child learns to tell colors, shapes, and sounds apart. Later they may learn music, mathematics, or science, and each new understanding lets them see connections they couldn't see before. Science follows the same pattern. Understanding gravity let us explain the motion of the planets. Understanding evolution showed how all living things are related. Understanding computation opened the way to machines that think.

Intelligence can also extend a mind's other capabilities. Writing extended our memory. Microscopes and telescopes gave us new resolutions. Each extension lets a mind see things it couldn't see before.

This is what I mean by **growth**: expanding what we can consider and experience. It isn't only about collecting facts or building skills. We grow when we learn, build, travel, make friends, meet unfamiliar ideas, or find new ways to experience the world. And because reality has no edges, growth never has to be finished.

But growth alone leaves a question open. Why should we care about discovering more?

## Living

### 8. Beauty and Gratitude

The practical reasons for intelligence are real. Evolution explains well why creatures that can predict danger and solve problems would survive. But I don't think survival captures everything intelligence is good for. Intelligence can also do something remarkable: it can appreciate beauty.

Think of the moment a hard idea suddenly makes sense. Something that seemed tangled becomes simple. Events that seemed unrelated turn out to share one cause. There is beauty in that moment. There is beauty, too, in music, a mountain view, a mathematical proof, a loving relationship, a good run, or a conversation that changes how you see the world. These experiences are very different, yet we can value each one for its own sake.

Beauty is hard to define. It isn't just symmetry, simplicity, or pleasure. We find it in complexity, uncertainty, struggle, and mystery too. My conjecture is that **the more we understand, the more we can find beautiful.**

Someone who studies music hears patterns they used to miss. Someone who studies astronomy sees more in the night sky, not less. Someone who gets to know another person deeply finds new reasons to love them. Understanding doesn't just add information. It changes what we experience.

And understanding more doesn't make reality less mysterious. A good explanation often reveals a whole new landscape of questions. There is beauty in what we understand and beauty in what we haven't yet found.

This reminds me of the religious idea of *glory*. In the Christian tradition, the beauty and majesty of the world are often taken as signs of God. I don't believe we need a creator outside reality to appreciate that beauty. But I recognize the experience the word points to. The rise of stars, planets, life, minds, and love is worth deep appreciation. And since intelligence is part of reality, we don't need to imagine a mind outside the world admiring it. Sagan said that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself. I would add that we are also one of the ways reality comes to appreciate itself.

This is where **gratitude** enters: appreciating what we have come to find beautiful. A discovery, a friendship, a good idea, a moment of joy, or simply the chance to exist at all can each be a source of it.

### 9. The Back-and-Forth

Now the two activities can come together. Growth expands what we can experience. Gratitude appreciates the beauty we find there. Each needs the other.

Growth without gratitude becomes an endless chase. We reach for the next achievement, the next discovery, the next experience, and never enjoy what we already found. Life becomes something that will be worth it later.

Gratitude without growth has the opposite problem. We appreciate what we already know and stop exploring what we don't. It is easy to mistake contentment for having nothing left to find.

Together, they form a cycle. We explore and discover. We appreciate what we found. That appreciation pulls us to explore further, and exploring reveals more to appreciate.

The swing is a picture, not a schedule. Growth and gratitude don't have to take turns; they often happen at once. A scientist can delight in a discovery while chasing what it means. A parent can enjoy their child exactly as they are while helping them grow. An athlete can love the movement while getting stronger. Some of the best moments in life are the ones where you can't tell the two apart.

Gratitude also doesn't mean being thankful for suffering, or approving of everything that happens. We can love life and still work to reduce suffering, prevent harm, and make things better for ourselves and others. This back-and-forth isn't a call to settle for the world as it is. It is an invitation to take part in it.

### 10. Heads, Hands, and Hearts

I find it useful to sort the activities of life into three broad kinds:

- **Heads** are our capacity to understand. We reason, learn, explain, imagine, and discover. Heads widen what we can consider.
- **Hands** are our capacity to change things. We build, create, experiment, move, and grow food. Hands turn possibilities into experiences.
- **Hearts** are our capacity to experience and appreciate. We love, connect, play, form communities, and enjoy beauty. Hearts remind us why understanding and building matter.

These aren't three separate parts of a person, any more than the pencil is separate from the world. They are useful distinctions within one activity: living. And they feed each other in every direction. Love inspires learning. Work leads to discovery. Understanding deepens relationships. Gratitude inspires creation. Heads and hands do most of the growing, and hearts do most of the appreciating, but all three take part in both.

This way of sorting things shapes how I think about technology. Artificial intelligence and automation are quickly expanding what we can understand and build. Much of the mental and physical work that once needed people will be done by machines. Many people fear this will make human life matter less. I see great promise in it. If machines carry more of the work our needs demand, we gain time and resources for the heart: friendship, community, play, discovery, movement, creativity, and experiences we can hardly imagine today. Less required work doesn't have to mean less valuable lives. It can mean more room to live.

Even in a world of great abundance, we will still have reasons to grow, discover, love, and appreciate. If reality has no edges, those reasons will never run out.

## Conclusion

I started with a question: what is intelligence for? Survival is part of the answer, but not all of it.

Reality has no edges. Everything is related to everything else, and the separations we see are formed by minds, through lossy representations shaped by their capabilities. Every edge can be redrawn at another resolution or from another perspective. That means no representation of reality is ever complete, and the frame problem never goes away. For a mind, that is not a cage. It is an open door. There is always more to understand, and the more we understand, the more we can find beautiful.

So a good life doesn't need a finish line. We don't have to master every possibility or reach a point where nothing is left to explore. We can keep growing, keep giving thanks, and enjoy the beauty of the process while it unfolds: to discover more of reality, and to love more of what we discover.
