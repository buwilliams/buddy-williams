# The Moving Line: AI's Compression Frontier and the Speed of Identity

## Introduction

Three claims about AI's economic impact are circulating right now, each made by serious people, and they appear to contradict one another.

The first is a repricing claim. Work that took a software consultancy three weeks — call it 120 billable hours — now takes six hours with frontier coding agents. That is a 95% reduction in time, a 20× speedup. Run the arithmetic on a delivery team of ten, billing at typical consulting rates at healthy utilization, and roughly $2.9M in annual revenue compresses toward $144K. On this view, firms must find twenty times the volume or watch their revenue collapse.

The second is an elasticity claim, stated most visibly by Jeff Bezos: AI will produce a labor shortage, not mass unemployment. The radiologists and software engineers everyone expects to disappear will instead be elevated — people who have been digging basements with shovels are about to be handed bulldozers. Productivity will rise so much that some households will choose to drop an earner, and core goods will get cheaper.

The third is a transformation-failure claim: the overwhelming majority of enterprise "AI transformation" initiatives fail, because organizations bolt AI pilots onto functional tasks without ever touching the business core, and much of existing leadership is unequipped for the transition.

Here is what makes this interesting: I do not think any of these claims is wrong. I think each one is a correct observation of a different variable, and the variables move at different speeds. The apparent contradiction dissolves once you stop asking "which claim is true?" and start asking "which clock is each observer watching?"

This essay develops that conjecture into a model, and the model turns on a single image. At any moment there is a line dividing the work AI performs from the work it does not. Every firm, meanwhile, has a boundary — the line dividing the work it sells from the work its clients keep. The first line moves at the speed of research. The second moves at the speed of a firm's identity, which will turn out to be the slowest thing in the entire system. The economic story of AI in professional services is the story of a race between these two lines — and almost everything that looks like a paradox in the three claims above dissolves into a statement about who can redraw their boundary faster than the frontier moves toward it.

I am not a disinterested observer. I run delivery teams at an enterprise software consultancy, and this model describes the ground shifting under my own feet. That is a reason to scrutinize the argument harder, not a reason to discount it; the essay ends by asking what moves the model actually licenses for someone standing where I stand — and it will not conclude that anyone, including me, gets to stand on solid ground.

As always, this is conjecture offered for criticism. Every load-bearing claim below is stated as a comparison of rates, and each of the model's predictions comes with a named condition that would refute it.

## 1. Rates, Not States

Most arguments about AI and jobs are arguments about states. "AI can write code, therefore programmers disappear." "Demand for software is unlimited, therefore programmers thrive." Both sentences compare a snapshot of capability to a snapshot of the labor market, and both are the wrong kind of claim, because the economy is not a snapshot. It is a system of processes running at different speeds, and what happens to any particular firm or worker depends on which processes reach them first.

So the method of this essay is to translate every static claim into a claim about rates. When someone says "AI does the work twenty times faster," the useful question is: twenty times faster than what, arriving how quickly, absorbed by whom, at what pace? This reframing sounds like a small move. It is not. It is the difference between predicting an equilibrium and predicting a path, and for anyone actually living through the transition — a firm, a worker, an industry — the path is the whole story. Equilibria do not send invoices.

To describe the path, I will use three axes along which business change actually travels. The first is **capability**: new technical possibility, such as agentic coding systems that can carry a feature from specification to working code. Capability moves at research speed — new model generations arrive in months. The second is **absorption**: the work an organization must do to convert a capability into functioning operations. Absorption is not installing software; it is building verification culture, deployment discipline, accountability structures, and redesigned workflows around the new capability. Absorption moves at organizational speed — years, because it runs on meetings, politics, budget cycles, and trust. The third is **distribution**: the spread of absorbed capability across the whole economy, firm by firm, industry by industry. Distribution has historically moved at economic speed — decades — though I will argue later that it may be compressing.

One clarification before we proceed: these axes are not stages in a sequence. Distribution does not politely wait for absorption to finish. Early distribution creates competitive pressure that forces laggard firms to absorb faster than they would have chosen — a feedback loop between the third axis and the second that will matter when we ask how long the transition takes.

A fourth axis will emerge in Section 7, and it will turn out to be the slowest and most decisive of all. But before any of the axes can do their work, the method itself — rates, not states — has to be applied to the one thing almost everyone treats as fixed.

## 2. The Moving Line

At any given moment, there is a line running through the world of work. On one side sits everything current AI systems can perform: today, that side has a recognizable profile — work that is specifiable, bounded, and verifiable, work you could hand to a competent stranger with a written brief and check without watching them do it. On the other side sits everything they cannot: work entangled with organizational context, ambiguity, politics, trust, and accountability. Call this line the **compression frontier**, because what crosses it gets compressed — the 120 hours becoming 6.

Nearly every confident claim about AI and work smuggles in the same hidden assumption: that the frontier is a fact about the *work*. It is not. It is a fact about the *models* — and models improve. Two years ago, writing production code sat comfortably on the human side of the line. Today it does not. Verification — reviewing code, writing tests, checking invariants — was, until recently, the standard example of what would remain human; agents now write the tests and run the harnesses. The frontier is not a property of tasks any more than a coastline is a property of the ocean floor. It is where the tide currently reaches, and the tide is coming in.

This means the method of Section 1 applies to the line itself. The wrong question about any kind of work is "is this compressible?" — a state question, and its answer expires. The right question is "how fast is the frontier moving toward it?" — a rate question, and the only kind that supports planning. Everything in this essay that looks like a static category — compressible versus serial work, depreciating versus durable advantage — should be read as a position relative to a moving line. Exposure is position; risk is the frontier's velocity toward you.

A line that moves raises the obvious question: does it ever stop? Is anything *permanently* on the human side? I think the answer is yes — but the boundary of the permanent lies somewhere different from where most discussions place it. Distinguish work that is **contingently** beyond the frontier from work that is **constitutively** beyond it. Contingent work is uncompressed only because the models have not reached it yet — verification, as we just saw, was contingent all along, however permanent it looked. Constitutive work, by contrast, is not a computation at all. It is a social or legal relation. Liability is the clearest case: "someone must be accountable when the system fails" is not a task a better model performs but a fact about courts, insurance, and the human need to trust before depending. Even where law adapts to machine-made decisions, the accountability does not vanish — it relocates to whoever deploys, insures, or certifies the system. The form changes; the relation persists. Trust between institutions, the legitimacy of a consequential decision, the assignment of responsibility: a model can *inform* these relations, but it cannot *be* a party to them. Call this set of work the **constitutive core**.

Two opposite errors haunt this distinction, and the essay will try to walk the ridge between them. Misclassifying contingent work as constitutive is the error that fills the graveyard of "AI will never X" predictions. Misclassifying constitutive work as contingent is the error that dissolves law, trust, and accountability into engineering problems they are not. How much of today's uncompressed work is truly constitutive — most of the residual, or a sliver — is, I believe, the single most important open question in this subject, and I return to it in Appendix C. What matters for now is that every "non-compressible" claim in the sections ahead carries this distinction inside it: either the claim names a relation, or it names a position the tide has merely not yet reached.

With the line and its limit in view, we can now watch what happens when the line sweeps across a market.

## 3. The Repricing Mechanism

Start with the napkin math from the introduction: a 120-hour task becomes a 6-hour task. (The arithmetic is laid out in Appendix A.) The naive reading says consulting revenue falls 95% unless volume rises 20×. The elasticity reading says volume *will* rise, because enterprises have roadmaps ten times longer than their budgets, and custom software may be the most demand-elastic good in the economy — every project that was uneconomical at $2.9M becomes thinkable at a fraction of that. Which reading is right?

Both — at different speeds. And here is the mechanism: **pricing responds to the frontier, but volume responds to absorption and distribution.** A competitor can quote AI-assisted prices tomorrow; price discovery only requires that someone in the market has crossed the line and wants your client's business. But volume expansion requires clients to absorb more software than they did before, and absorption is gated by change management, data readiness, compliance review, and organizational will — all of which move at firm speed, not model speed. An insurance carrier does not integrate twenty new systems this year merely because systems got cheap.

So the price falls fast and the volume arrives slowly, and the interval between them is what I will call the **interregnum**: the period in which the old revenue model is dead and the new demand has not yet materialized. The repricing claim correctly describes the interregnum. The elasticity claim correctly describes the equilibrium beyond it. Neither is a complete account, because each is watching a different clock — a pattern we will see again.

The interregnum has a corollary that I think is underappreciated: the first casualty is not the consultancy but the *pricing model*. Time-and-materials billing works only while input time tracks output value. Once the frontier decouples them, hourly billing becomes unstable regardless of what any individual firm decides — a firm that keeps selling hours into a market that has repriced hours is volunteering for the exposed class. The interesting question is therefore not "how do we do twenty times the volume?" but "how do we stop selling time?" And answering that question requires knowing exactly where each firm sits relative to the line — which brings us to Amdahl.

## 4. The Amdahl Inversion

Amdahl's Law comes from parallel computing, and it says something simple: the total speedup of a system is bounded by the fraction of work that cannot be parallelized. If half your workload is inherently serial, infinite processors buy you at most a 2× speedup. Applied to consulting, the standard move is reassuring: coding compresses 20×, but discovery, stakeholder alignment, verification, and deployment do not, so the engagement as a whole speeds up far less than the headline number, and the revenue collapse is overstated.

I want to argue that this reassurance is wrong at the level of the firm and correct at the level of the economy — and that the inversion is the single most clarifying idea in this model.

Consider the firm level first. The reassuring argument quietly assumes that every consultancy carries a healthy serial fraction of non-compressible work. But a firm's mix of work is not handed down by nature; it is chosen, shaped by decades of contracts and client relationships. And in my own corner of the industry, the historical division of labor is stark: clients kept the serial work — planning, requirements, alignment, politics — and outsourced the specifiable core, the architecture and implementation. In many engagements the client-retained bottlenecks account for a small fraction of billable time, perhaps a tenth or less. Why did the boundary land there? Because contracts govern best what can be specified, bounded, and verified — and so, over decades, the market naturally assigned consultancies the specifiable work.

Now recall the frontier's profile from Section 2: it advances first through work that is specifiable, bounded, and verifiable. The very properties that made implementation outsourceable make it automatable. This is not bad luck. The client–consultancy boundary was drawn, long ago, along precisely the frontier's *current* position — the firm's boundary and the compression line coincide, which means the frontier's next step forward lands entirely inside the consultancy's territory. An implementation-pure consultancy is not partially exposed; it is maximally exposed *by construction*, because its portfolio was selected — by the logic of contracting itself — to contain almost nothing but the compressible fraction. At the firm level, Amdahl's Law does not bind; a firm can sit entirely on the wrong side of the line, and many do. The serial fraction is, in the economist's sense, **endogenous**: a variable the firm's history has chosen, not a constant imposed on it.

At the level of the whole economy, the situation reverses. Someone, somewhere, must do the serial work — establishing trust, satisfying regulators, changing organizations, holding accountability. At the macro level the serial fraction is **exogenous** — no one gets to choose it away — and Amdahl binds hard. This, I conjecture, is the deep reason absorption and distribution are slow: the economy's serial fraction is exactly the absorption work of Section 1, and it runs at organizational speed no matter how fast the models improve.

Put the two levels together, carrying Section 2's distinction, and you get the model's conservation law: **serial work is conserved and relocated only insofar as it is constitutive; everything contingently serial is on borrowed time, at a rate set by the frontier's velocity.** The economy's serial fraction is exogenous at every moment — no one gets to choose it away today — but it is not constant across moments: the frontier annexes the contingent portion generation by generation, and much of what I have been calling absorption may prove contingent too — an uncomfortable possibility the model must carry rather than hide. What the frontier never annexes is the constitutive core, which is why the core is where the residual ultimately concentrates: relocation has a destination, and the destination is trust, liability, and accountability.

The conservation law makes the strategic question of the interregnum precise: as contingently serial work is annexed and the residual relocates toward the constitutive core, who captures it? A natural answer suggests itself immediately: the consultancy should simply move its boundary — stop selling implementation, start selling the residual. Hold that thought. It is correct as far as it goes, and Section 7 will show why it goes much less far than it appears. But first there is a second force reshaping the market, one that does not merely reprice work but removes it from the market altogether.

## 5. The Coasean Floor

Ronald Coase asked why firms exist at all — why any work happens inside organizations rather than being purchased, task by task, on the open market. His answer was transaction costs: using the market is not free. Finding a counterparty, negotiating terms, verifying quality, enforcing the agreement — all of this costs something, and when it costs more than doing the work in-house, the work stays in-house.

Every consulting engagement carries these costs in concrete form: procurement processes, master service agreements, security reviews, onboarding, access provisioning. Call their sum the engagement's **transaction-cost floor**. The crucial property of this floor is that it is roughly fixed per engagement — a six-week procurement cycle costs the same whether it precedes six months of work or six hours of it.

Now run the compression from Section 3 against this floor. When the production work inside an engagement shrinks 20×, the fixed transaction costs come to dominate the total cost of engaging outside help. And past some threshold, the rational client does not respond by negotiating a lower price. The client stops contracting for that class of work entirely and does it in-house, because no price makes a six-week procurement cycle sensible for a six-hour task.

This is the mechanism I want to name clearly, because it is easy to mistake for repricing when it is something structurally different: the low end of the consulting market does not reprice — it **evaporates**. Work falls below the floor and exits the market. I am already watching the early form of this: clients running the same coding agents internally and asking, reasonably, why they need us. For the moment the answer is that we use the agents better than they do. The next section will examine how long that answer survives.

Evaporation, not repricing, is why the right historical comparison for consulting is travel agencies rather than radiologists. Radiologists faced a capability threat and absorbed the capability. Travel agencies faced a transaction-cost collapse — booking a flight yourself became easier than engaging an intermediary — and the market for their core product simply ceased to exist, leaving only the high-complexity remnant (corporate travel, luxury itineraries) that stayed above the floor. The consulting products that survive evaporation will share that profile: engagements too large, too risky, or too organizationally entangled to fall below the floor — transformation programs, regulated-system accountability, embedded capacity. Notice what these have in common: they cluster around the constitutive core from Section 2 — trust, liability, accountability — not artifacts. The floor independently rediscovers the frontier's residual.

One refinement matters for reconciling our three original claims. Repricing and evaporation strike the *same* firms, but the volume, when elasticity finally delivers it, arrives to *different* parties — and substantially to non-firms, meaning work that clients now perform internally and that never re-enters the external market at all. The Jevons-style recovery is real at the level of the economy and partially illusory at the level of the industry. This is how Bezos can be right about labor in aggregate while the consulting industry specifically has a very bad decade. Aggregate optimism and industry devastation are not in tension; they are the same event viewed from different distances.

How high the floor sits, and how it moves over time, is genuinely uncertain — the frontier may compress transaction costs too, a possibility taken up in Appendix C. But whatever its height, a firm's ability to stay above it depends on what kind of advantages the firm holds. The moving line gives that question a geometric answer.

## 6. Advantage as Distance from the Line

My firm's current answer to "why do clients still need us?" is that we use coding agents better than our clients do. This is true today, and the frontier framework reveals exactly what kind of truth it is: an advantage located *just* on the human side of the line, in the frontier's direct path. Every capability generation is built, deliberately, to require less skill from its user — the labs optimize for the marginal user, not the expert, which means each release raises the floor faster than the ceiling. Prompt engineering was a genuine differentiator with a half-life of perhaps eighteen months before the tide reached it. Orchestration skill — coordinating multiple agents, building verification harnesses — stands a little further up the beach, but on the same slope.

This suggests a classification every professional-services firm should run on its own balance sheet, and the moving line makes it quantitative in spirit even where it cannot be quantitative in fact: **an advantage's half-life is its distance from the frontier divided by the frontier's velocity.** Tool-use skill of every kind — anything of the form "we are better at operating the capability" — sits nearest the line, because operating the capability is precisely what each model generation absorbs next. What sits far from the line? Only things whose distance is not measured in task difficulty at all. **Proprietary context**: knowledge of a client's systems, data, politics, and history — in no training set, expensive to transfer, and replenished faster than it leaks. **Liability and trust**: being the accountable party — constitutively serial, in Section 2's terms, and therefore at infinite distance from a line that only crosses computations. **Organizational position**: being inside a client's change process rather than a vendor to it. And **distribution**: owning the relationship through which capability flows to the client at all. Note the pattern: the durable advantages are not skills but *relations* — positions in a web of trust, knowledge, and access — which is exactly what the constitutive core predicted they would be.

Now the uncomfortable part. Look at where each class of asset accumulates by default. Tool skill accumulates in whoever uses the tools — for now, the consultancy. But context, trust, and position accumulate in whoever does the serial work — and Section 4 established that, under the historical division of labor, the serial work lives inside the client. The implementation-shaped consultancy has spent decades accumulating the asset nearest the frontier while its clients accumulated the ones farthest from it. This is the Amdahl inversion restated as an asset ledger, and it converts a strategy question into a solvency question: the firm's remaining advantage has a half-life, the frontier's velocity is not under the firm's control, and the clock is running.

The prescription seems to write itself: move up the ledger. Sell context, trust, and position instead of implementation. Redraw the firm's boundary ahead of the advancing line. I said at the end of Section 4 that this answer goes less far than it appears. Here is why.

## 7. The Double Bind: Value Networks and Identity

Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* is usually summarized as "incumbents miss the new thing," which misses his actual finding. Christensen showed that incumbents see the disruption clearly and respond *rationally* — and that the rationality is what kills them. A firm's **value network** — its web of existing customers, pricing structures, sales motions, and margin expectations — evaluates every strategic option, and it reliably scores the sustaining option above the disruptive one. For a consultancy, the value network's counsel is: use AI to deliver implementations faster at better margin. This is a genuinely rational response, and it is a melting-ice-cube strategy — improving margins on territory the frontier is actively annexing. The disruptive response — invading the client's serial territory to sell discovery, absorption, and organizational rearchitecting — is resisted not because anyone fails to see it but because it means a different buyer, a different sales motion, a different margin structure, and the existing network votes against all three.

That is the first layer of the bind, and if it were the only layer, a sufficiently determined leadership could override it. The second layer is the one I have come to think is decisive, and it is where the fourth axis promised in Section 1 finally enters.

Call it **identity** — and I mean something specific, with an external and an internal face. Externally, identity is brand: the compressed prior in the client's head about what your firm is *for*. A brand that has meant "hand them a spec, get back working software in a regulated environment" for twenty years cannot be re-indexed to "let them into our planning, our politics, our org chart" by a repositioned website. The client's procurement function does not update its priors from marketing. Internally, identity is culture — and culture is not the mission statement but the *selection function*: the accumulated pattern of who gets hired, promoted, and retained. A delivery culture selects, year after year, for people who excel at shipping against specifications. The people who would thrive in ambiguous, political, artifact-light absorption work are largely people that selection function filtered out long ago. The firm that decides to sell absorption discovers it no longer employs the people who could.

Identity, then, is the fourth clock, and here the essay's two lines meet. The compression frontier moves at research speed. A firm's boundary — what it sells, who it employs, what its name means — moves at identity speed, which I will call generational, because meaningfully changing a selection function means changing the population it has selected. **Survival, for a firm, is keeping its boundary ahead of a line that moves faster than the firm can redraw.** The double bind is now geometric: the value network votes against redrawing, and even a firm that overrules the vote discovers that its pen — its brand and its people — writes at a speed the frontier outruns.

Christensen's own data supplies the empirical anchor, and it is grim. Incumbents essentially never reposition into the disruptive segment from inside the existing organization. The rare survivors did it through autonomous spin-outs — separate P&L, separate brand, and, tellingly, separate *hiring*. The case usually offered as the counterexample, IBM's transformation into a services company under Gerstner, required a decade, a near-death experience, and an outsider CEO — which reads to me less like a refutation than like a statement of the price.

This yields what I will call, half-seriously, the insight-irrelevance theorem: **in identity-bound transitions, insight is not the missing ingredient, and supplying more of it does not unblock the transition.** The fix is not executable from inside the existing identity — not by better analysis, not by more conviction, not at any level of seniority — because the constraint is the speed of identity itself, not the availability of knowledge. I want to flag this as one of the boldest conjectures in the essay, stated more absolutely than the evidence strictly forces; perhaps some mechanism can compress identity change short of near-death (Appendix C takes this up). But I have watched enough transformation initiatives to believe the strong version, and it explains something the weaker versions cannot: why the failure rate of enterprise AI transformation stays above 90% *even as understanding of AI spreads*. The claim that half of all companies need new leadership actually understates the problem. They need new identities, and leadership swaps are attempted precisely because identity swaps are not on the menu.

## 8. Two Lines, Four Clocks

The model is now complete, and it can be stated as a single race. One line — the compression frontier — advances through the territory of work at research speed, annexing first whatever is specifiable, bounded, and verifiable, and slowing only as it approaches the constitutive core of trust, liability, and accountability. Against it, every actor in the economy holds a boundary, and every actor redraws that boundary at a characteristic speed. Incumbent firms redraw at identity speed — generational, the slowest in the system, which is why the frontier catches them. Client organizations redraw at absorption speed — years — which is why the volume is slow and the transformation failures are many. New entrants do not redraw at all in the usual sense: they *draw*, once, placing their boundary along the frontier's current position — which is precisely their advantage, and precisely its expiration mechanism, since the frontier keeps moving after the founding documents are signed. And individuals redraw at what I will call identity-speed-of-one: a person can walk across a boundary in months that a firm cannot cross in a decade.

Read this way, the four clocks of the model — capability, absorption, distribution, identity — are not four separate phenomena. They are the answers to one question asked of four different actors: *how fast can you redraw your boundary?* And every one of the confident public claims from the introduction is a correct reading of one actor's clock. The repricing pessimists read the frontier's clock and are right that prices collapse at market speed; they miss the absorption clock, which delays the volume but does not cancel it. Bezos reads the frontier and distribution clocks and is right about the equilibrium — the elevation, the elasticity, the eventual abundance; he is not reading the identity clock, which determines *who survives to see* the equilibrium. The transformation critics read the absorption clock and correctly count the failures; they attribute to leadership what belongs to identity, one clock further down. Each layer's lag is invisible to optimists watching the layer above it.

The sequence of the interregnum then falls out of the clock speeds. First, the frontier annexes the specifiable core of professional work — fast. Second, pricing collapses on hour-denominated work, at market speed, because pricing needs only one competitor across the line. Third, the Coasean floor evaporates the low end of the market as clients in-house whatever falls below it, paced by the clients' own absorption speed. Fourth, the residual relocates toward the constitutive core — into clients, and to new entrants selling absorption — but not to incumbent consultancies, whose boundaries cannot be redrawn in time. Fifth, and slowest, elasticity delivers the promised volume — to different parties than the ones who were repriced. The conservation law of Section 4 guarantees that the constitutive residual is captured by someone; the race of boundary against frontier determines by whom.

A model assembled this neatly should make a reader suspicious, and the correct response to suspicion is to ask what the model forbids. Here is what it forbids.

## 9. Three Predictions

**First: some agencies fail — and the model says which ones and how.** The firms that fail first are implementation-pure agencies in weakly regulated verticals, priced in hours, holding no assets distant from the frontier — no context, no constitutive trust, no position. And the failure mode is specific: not the gradual margin compression a repricing story predicts, but discontinuous *evaporation* — whole classes of engagement vanishing from the pipeline as clients in-house work that fell below the Coasean floor. Agencies in regulated verticals survive longer, held above the floor by trust and liability; but the model insists this is duration, not immunity, unless the borrowed time is converted into assets farther from the line. This prediction is refuted if implementation-pure agencies broadly maintain revenue through the transition by volume expansion alone, without changing what they sell.

**Second: new entrants win — for a time, and the frontier itself sets the expiration.** AI-native vendors, and in particular their forward-deployed engineering functions, capture the relocated residual, because they arrive with no prior to overwrite: their brand already reads "we are the capability," and their boundary is drawn fresh along the frontier's current position. They enter the absorption market as greenfield players rather than as reformed consultancies fighting their own identity. But "for a time" is not a hedge — it is a derivation, and the moving line supplies the mechanism. The entrant's boundary, once drawn, begins aging the moment the frontier moves again. Today's AI-native absorption product is tomorrow's implementation shop, because much of absorption work is contingently serial: the frontier that annexed coding and then verification will press into deployment discipline, workflow redesign, and the translation of organizational intent into system behavior. The entrant's founding advantage is a *position*; only continuous redrawing — a capability entrants possess in youth and lose as their own identities harden into incumbency — converts it into survival. This prediction is refuted if incumbent consultancies, without spin-outs, capture the majority of the absorption market.

**Third: client-side workers have the strongest upside of any party in the model.** Look at where the frontier-distant assets accumulate by default: proprietary context, organizational position, and trust all pool *inside* client organizations, in the people doing the serial work. The client-side worker who absorbs the capability gets Bezos's bulldozer and keeps the context, holds the position, and faces no Coasean floor, no brand prior, no procurement cycle — the frictions that doom the external firm simply do not apply to an insider. The elevation thesis is most true, soonest, for exactly this population. Their binding constraint is their own organization's absorption speed — but unlike firms, individuals can redraw at identity-speed-of-one, which is the hinge of the final section. This prediction is refuted if capability gains accrue predominantly to external vendors while client-side technical roles stagnate or contract.

## 10. What Remains Executable

The model closes on a personal question, and I will not pretend it is hypothetical. If the insight-irrelevance theorem is right, then the person inside an incumbent consultancy who sees all of this clearly — the person whose job title says they are responsible for exactly this transition — cannot fix it from where they stand. Not because they lack authority or conviction, but because the constraint was never insight. What moves does the model actually license?

I count two.

**The first is to relocate across the boundary at identity-speed-of-one.** The identity clock binds firms because firms are selection functions with decades of momentum. It does not bind individuals the same way: a person can cross to the side where the frontier-distant assets accumulate — vendor-side roles positioned inside the capability flow, accumulating proprietary context across many clients' absorption problems — in months. This reframes a question that deserves a principled answer: why would someone who sees the whole model leave rather than fix the firm? Because Christensen's data says the fix is not executable from inside, by anyone, at any level of insight. Leaving is not an abandonment of the thesis. Under the strong version of the theorem, it is the only individual-level move the thesis licenses — the third prediction, applied reflexively, by someone arbitraging their own model.

**The second is a new company under a new brand.** This is the Christensen-compliant escape for a group rather than an individual: an autonomous entity with a separate P&L and a brand carrying no implementation prior — the entrant advantage of the second prediction, claimed deliberately rather than stumbled into — selling the residual product from day one, in the regulated verticals where the Coasean floor sits highest and holds longest.

Honesty requires two caveats, and the essay's own logic forces both.

The first concerns the second path. "Same people, new brand" satisfies the brand half of the identity condition and only partially satisfies the culture half — because culture travels with people, which is exactly why Christensen's surviving spin-outs used separate hiring, not just separate letterhead. A new brand staffed entirely by implementation-selected people carries the old selection function inward even as it escapes the old prior outward. The model therefore predicts that the second path attempted as *the old firm with a new logo* fails by the same mechanism as the incumbents — and that it succeeds only as a genuine re-founding, in which the team's composition is re-selected against the new product: absorption work, ambiguity tolerance, political fluency, rather than delivery excellence alone.

The second caveat concerns the first path, and it is the one the moving line itself forces. The moving line does not stop at the boundary I would be crossing. If much of absorption work is contingently rather than constitutively serial — and I argued in the second prediction that it is — then the frontier that annexed implementation will, in time, press into forward-deployed engineering too. The vendor-side role is not a safe harbor. It is a *position*: closer to the constitutive core than implementation, farther from the line, with a longer half-life — but a half-life all the same. The honest statement of the model is that **nobody in it stands on solid ground.** There is no occupation, firm, or strategy in this essay whose durability is guaranteed; there are only positions relative to a moving line and speeds at which different actors can redraw. The individual's advantage is not safety. It is possessing the fastest redraw speed in the system — and being willing to use it more than once.

Which path is better? The model even locates the decision variable: the height and durability of the Coasean floor in the regulated verticals. If the floor stays high — if trust and liability keep transaction costs rigid while production costs collapse — there is a defensible external market for the residual, and the second path has ground to build on. If the floor falls — if the frontier compresses procurement, verification, and inter-firm trust the way it compressed implementation — the external market evaporates from below, and the first path is the honest one: be where the capability is, not where the contracts were. I do not yet know which way the floor moves, and I have tried to say so plainly rather than pick the answer that flatters either choice.

## Conclusion

I opened with three claims that appeared to contradict one another: consulting revenue collapses twentyfold; AI produces labor shortage and elevation, not unemployment; and the vast majority of AI transformations fail. The model developed here says all three are correct observations of the same race, watched from different positions. A compression frontier advances through the territory of work at research speed. Every actor holds a boundary and redraws it at a characteristic speed — firms at generational identity speed, organizations at absorption speed, entrants at founding speed, individuals at identity-speed-of-one. The repricing claim reads the frontier. The elasticity claim reads the equilibrium beyond it. The transformation-failure claim reads the absorption clock and — without quite knowing it — is measuring the identity clock beneath it, the slowest and least visible variable in the system.

Along the way, the model produced what I think are its load-bearing ideas. The compression frontier is a fact about models, not about work, and it moves — so every claim of "non-compressible" is a rate claim in disguise, and only the constitutive core of trust, liability, and accountability is conserved in principle rather than merely unreached in practice. Serial work relocates toward that core; Amdahl's Law binds the economy but not the firm, which is why repricing is fast, volume is slow, and exposure is positional. The low end of the consulting market evaporates rather than reprices, because compression pushes work below the fixed transaction costs of engaging anyone at all. An advantage's half-life is its distance from the frontier divided by the frontier's velocity, and the durable advantages are relations, not skills. And identity, not insight, is the binding constraint on incumbent adaptation, which is why the failure rate persists even as understanding spreads, and why the residual flows to entrants and insiders rather than to reformed incumbents.

If the model is right, its deepest irony is that the transition is hardest on precisely the organizations built to sell technical change, and kindest to the client-side workers everyone expected it to displace. The bulldozer goes to the person standing in the basement. And its deepest discomfort is that the model exempts no one — not the entrants whose founding advantage ages from the day it is drawn, not the vendor-side engineer whose role sits merely farther up the same beach, not the author. Nobody stands on solid ground.

I do not find this conclusion bleak, and I want to end by saying why, because the reason is not consolation — it is the same epistemology the essay runs on. The moving frontier is not only an annexing force; it is a generative one. Every position it consumes, it consumed by creating capabilities that did not exist before, and those capabilities open problems, products, and roles that could not have been specified in advance — any more than "forward-deployed engineer" could have been specified five years ago, or "software consultant" fifty years before that. We navigate by conjecture: we can only pursue the opportunities we can currently see, knowing the map will be redrawn, and knowing that the redrawing is precisely where the new opportunities come from. The demand that our positions be permanent is a demand the world has never honored for anyone. What the world does offer — and what a fallibilist should ask of it — is the boundlessness of problems worth solving. Solid ground was never the offer. Motion was.

I hold all of this fallibly. The insight-irrelevance theorem is stated more strongly than the case law strictly proves; the size of the constitutive core is an open empirical question; the trajectory of the Coasean floor is another; and the graveyard of "the models will never do X" predictions counsels humility about every residual I have located, however carefully, beyond the line. The predictions in Section 9 come with their refutation conditions attached on purpose. If implementation-pure agencies sail through unchanged, if incumbents capture absorption without re-founding, or if client-side workers fail to inherit the elevation — the model is wrong, and I want to know it before the interregnum teaches the lesson the expensive way.

## Appendix A: The Repricing Arithmetic

The compression figures used in the essay, for a representative engagement and delivery organization.

Task compression: original effort *o* = 120 hours (three weeks); new effort *n* = 6 hours. Remaining fraction *r* = *n* ÷ *o* = 0.05, i.e., 5% of original time, a 95% reduction, a compression ratio *k* = *o* ÷ *n* = 20.

Revenue exposure: a team of 10, at approximately 60% billable utilization across a 48-week year, at $250/hour, produces roughly 10 × 0.60 × 48 × 40 × $250 ≈ $2.9M annually. Under full 20× compression of the billable work at unchanged hourly pricing, the same delivered scope yields approximately $144K.

Two corrections from the essay apply to this arithmetic. First, the compression applies to the fraction of engagements behind the frontier, not to engagements whole — though Section 4 argues that for implementation-pure firms that fraction approaches the entire book, making the naive figure closer to correct than the standard Amdahl objection suggests. Second, the arithmetic assumes hourly pricing survives the transition; Section 3 argues it does not, which makes the calculation a statement about the instability of the pricing model rather than a forecast of any particular firm's revenue.

## Appendix B: Key Terms

**Compression frontier.** The moving line dividing work AI systems currently perform from work they do not. A fact about models, not about work; its current profile favors work that is specifiable, bounded, and verifiable. Exposure is position relative to the frontier; risk is the frontier's velocity toward that position.

**Contingently serial.** Work that is uncompressed only because the frontier has not reached it yet (e.g., verification until recently; likely much of absorption work).

**Constitutively serial.** Work that is uncompressible in principle because it is a social or legal relation rather than a computation: liability, accountability, trust between institutions, the legitimacy of consequential decisions. The **constitutive core** (Section 2) is the set of such work, and its true size is the model's most important open question.

**Capability (first axis).** New technical possibility; moves at research speed (months per generation). The frontier's advance is the capability clock made spatial.

**Absorption (second axis).** The organizational work of converting capability into functioning operations — verification culture, deployment discipline, accountability structures, workflow redesign; moves at organizational speed (years).

**Distribution (third axis).** The spread of absorbed capability across the economy; moves at economic speed (years to decades), with a feedback loop by which early distribution pressures laggards into faster absorption.

**Identity (fourth axis).** A firm's brand (the compressed prior in the client's mind about what the firm is for) and culture (the selection function determining who is hired, promoted, and retained); moves at generational speed, the slowest clock in the system. A firm's boundary is redrawn at identity speed; survival is keeping the boundary ahead of the frontier.

**Identity-speed-of-one.** The redraw speed available to an individual, who can cross a boundary in months that a firm cannot cross in a decade; the fastest redraw speed in the model.

**Interregnum.** The interval between fast price compression and slow volume arrival, during which the old revenue model is dead and the new demand has not yet materialized.

**Conservation law.** Serial work is conserved and relocated only insofar as it is constitutive; contingently serial work is annexed by the frontier over time. The serial fraction is endogenous (chosen) at the firm level and exogenous (binding, at any given moment) at the economy level.

**Coasean floor.** The fixed transaction costs of any external engagement — procurement, contracting, security review, onboarding — below which work exits the market entirely (evaporation) rather than repricing.

**Advantage half-life.** An advantage's distance from the frontier divided by the frontier's velocity. Tool skill sits nearest the line; the durable advantages — proprietary context, liability and trust, organizational position, distribution — are relations rather than skills, located at or near the constitutive core.

**Insight-irrelevance theorem.** In identity-bound transitions, insight is not the missing ingredient; the transition is not executable from inside the existing identity, by anyone, at any level of understanding.

## Appendix C: Open Problems

**The size of the constitutive core.** How much of the residual — absorption, trust work, accountability, judgment — is constitutively serial rather than merely unreached? This is the question on which every durability claim in the model depends. Misclassifying contingent as constitutive repeats the "AI will never X" error; misclassifying constitutive as contingent treats law, trust, and institutional legitimacy as engineering problems, which they are not. The strongest candidate for a genuinely constitutive *skill* (as opposed to relation) is taste under uncertainty — judgment about which conjectures are worth the compute — and I hold it loosely, because it may only be contingent work standing farther up the beach.

**The trajectory of the Coasean floor.** The model's market-structure predictions — and the choice between its two executable paths — hinge on whether the frontier compresses transaction costs (procurement, verification, inter-firm trust) as quickly as it compresses production costs. If transaction costs fall in tandem, small-scale market exchange survives and implementation work gig-ifies rather than in-housing. If trust and liability hold transaction costs rigid, the in-housing wave dominates. The answer likely differs by industry, with regulated verticals holding the floor highest longest. Note that this question is the constitutive-core question in disguise: transaction costs are rigid exactly insofar as they are constitutive.

**The frontier's velocity and shape.** The model treats the frontier as advancing along a single gradient (specifiable → ambiguous). Is that right, or does it advance unevenly — leaping past work assumed safe while stalling on work assumed doomed? An uneven frontier would break the "distance ÷ velocity" half-life heuristic and demand a richer geometry.

**The strength of the distribution–absorption feedback.** How much does competitive pressure from early adopters accelerate laggard absorption? Strong feedback shortens the interregnum, making the repricing sharper but briefer, and shrinks the entrant window of the second prediction. The electrification precedent (roughly forty years from clearly superior capability to realized productivity, because factories were architected around the steam shaft) suggests a long lag; the feedback loop is the main reason to expect this diffusion to run faster.

**Compressing the identity clock.** Are there mechanisms that move identity faster than generational speed — deliberately, rather than through near-death and outsider leadership as in the IBM case? If such mechanisms exist and can be invoked on purpose, the insight-irrelevance theorem weakens from a law to a strong tendency, and some incumbents escape. I know of no clean example, which is evidence, but not proof, that the strong version holds.
