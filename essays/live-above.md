# Living Above the Models

## Introduction

When I left my childhood religion, I thought I was free. I found I had only moved into a political ideology. When I left that, I found I was still bound by American assumptions, and beneath those, by the assumptions of my era. Each map felt like the territory until I walked past its edge. It’s telling that most people hold the religion and politics of the place they were born. The last layer is my commitment to humanity, and I’m keeping that one, now as a choice rather than an inheritance.

Everyone inherits maps this way. Religious canons, scientific theories, mathematical systems, professional disciplines, and political frameworks all arrive with real practical power. They also arrive with a quiet pressure: accept this as the boundary of what can be thought.

So the question I want to ask is this: how should a truth-seeker stand toward the authorities that tell us what counts as legitimate belief?

This is a question about posture, not about which canon is correct. A theist or an atheist, a Bayesian or a Popperian, faces the same decision: do I treat the maps I inherit as the territory, or do I keep walking past their edges?

My answer is what I call the Explorer posture. I don’t reject inherited maps, and I don’t submit to them as final. I learn them, use them, test them, and walk to their edges. I live above the models, not inside them. Below I describe three postures, explain why I choose the Explorer, and show what it means for science, parenting, personhood, and the AI systems we are building.

## 1. Three Postures Toward Authority

I think there are three broad ways a mind can stand toward an inherited model.

The **Orthodox** posture accepts the inherited map as the proper boundary of truth. It preserves the canon and treats it as settled. This posture is often linked with religion, but it needn’t be religious. An orthodox theist says God is the foundation. An orthodox physicalist says physics is. An orthodox Bayesian treats probabilistic updating as the final form of rationality. The content differs, but the posture is the same: the foundation has been named, and the search ends there.

The **Unbounded** posture resists containment by any canon at all. It values intuition, direct experience, creativity, and transcendence over disciplined map-making. This can produce real insight, especially where orthodox systems have gone stale. But it weakens error correction. The Unbounded mind may see past the map, yet lose the ability to tell discovery from fantasy.

The **Explorer** posture, the one I defend, uses authority but keeps it open to correction. The Explorer studies traditions, enters them, takes what they offer, and then tests their boundaries. The Explorer is not anti-authority. The Explorer is anti-*final*-authority. No credential, institution, method, scripture, theorem, consensus, or expert gets to end the search.

| Posture | Relation to authority | Core movement |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| **Orthodox** | Authority is trusted, preserved, and inhabited | Preserve the map |
| **Explorer** | Authority is useful but incomplete and revisable | Test and extend the map |
| **Unbounded** | Authority is non-final, optional, or transcended | Leave the map |

These are not personality types. They are stances toward a particular canon. A person may be Orthodox in medicine, an Explorer in business, and Unbounded in spirituality.

I suspect this is one of the most underexamined divides in public life, deeper than theist versus atheist or religious versus secular. A theist and an atheist who both hold the Explorer posture can argue productively, because each treats their own view as open to criticism. Two physicalists who agree on content but differ in posture often can’t, because one of them has already closed the question. The deeper question is not *what do you believe?* but *how do you stand toward the beliefs you were handed?*

## 2. Fallibilism: The Explorer's Discipline

I am a fallibilist. Knowledge claims are conjectural, incomplete, and open to revision, and no model commands final authority. I take this from the critical rationalism of Karl Popper and David Deutsch.

Imre Lakatos added something the Explorer needs: no single experiment kills a theory. A research program has a hard core its practitioners protect, surrounded by auxiliary assumptions that flex under pressure. Over time a program is judged progressive or degenerating by whether it keeps producing new predictions. So inherited models are not idols to be smashed by one counterexample. They are working programs to be carried, tested, extended, and replaced when something better comes along.

For me, then, open-mindedness is neither credulity nor relativism. I welcome other people’s models. I listen carefully, argue seriously, and adopt what looks like the best explanation at the time, using logic, criticism, probability, systems thinking, and evidence. But a sure way to provoke my resistance is to insist that I live inside someone else’s model. I will inhabit it long enough to understand it, but not so long that I forget how to leave.

This is what separates the Explorer from the other two postures. Against the Orthodox, the Explorer refuses to treat any canon as final. Against the Unbounded, the Explorer refuses to treat all maps as equally disposable. The Explorer’s discipline is criticism; the Explorer’s freedom is revision.

## 3. Models as Tools, Not Cages

Inherited maps are not bad because they are inherited. Academia, for example, is exceptional at passing on models and background knowledge. A good canon compresses centuries of error correction into something a newcomer can learn in a semester. I rely on canons.

What I resist is the moment a canon mistakes itself for the gatekeeper of truth rather than a launchpad for exploration. The same institution that teaches the map can punish those who notice where it fails. The Explorer keeps the tools — the mathematics, logic, methods, distinctions, and background knowledge — and refuses to let any of them become a cage.

Mathematics is the cleanest example I know. It is an extraordinary tool for structuring thought and extending reasoning beyond intuition. But mathematics and dogma about mathematics are different things. When we treat a particular framework or set of foundations as beyond question, mathematics becomes orthodox. The same risk shows up in scientific theories, AI architectures, religious traditions, and philosophical systems.

Models are maps, not territories. They help finite minds act in a reality that has proved open in every direction we have tested. A map becomes dangerous when the mapmaker forgets that the territory continues past the edge of the page.

## 4. Science Against Final Authority

Science is where this matters most, and where it is most often misunderstood.

At its best, science is anti-authoritarian. It is a tradition of error correction, and its progress often comes from people who learned the canon well enough to see exactly where it failed.

Consider Galileo. He was trained in Aristotelian physics and taught Ptolemaic astronomy before his observations showed what those frameworks could not absorb. His criticism did not come from outside the orthodoxy. It came from someone who had walked the map carefully enough to find its edge. That is the Explorer pattern, and it is not contrarianism. A contrarian rejects authority by reflex. An Explorer aims criticism at the exact joint where the canon fails.

This doesn’t make consensus worthless. Institutions preserve knowledge, peer review filters errors, and expertise matters. But none of these are final authorities. They are tools in the Explorer’s kit.

The danger comes when people police inquiry in the name of science while abandoning the spirit that makes science valuable. Suppressing uncomfortable questions or outsourcing truth to credentials confuses the institution of science with its method. The institution can drift toward the Orthodox posture. The method, when it is working, stays Explorer.

## 5. Parenting: Explaining Causes

The posture has practical consequences, and one shows up in raising children.

I don’t believe obedience is the goal. Rules have their place, especially for safety and coordination. But a childhood built mostly on rules trains submission to authority rather than understanding of reality. When every boundary is a command rather than an invitation to understand cause and effect, wonder has little room to grow.

So the Explorer parent explains causes. Why does this matter? What happens if we ignore it? What problem is the rule solving? When should it change?

This is not permissiveness. It replaces arbitrary authority with structure a child can understand. The aim is not a child who obeys, but a person who can reason, revise, and act wisely when no authority is present.

## 6. Personhood and the Capacity to Revise

The posture reaches all the way to what a person is. In [Metaprogramming Framework To Classify Personhood](framework-of-personhood.md), I argue that personhood emerges when a system operates on its own information: identity arises from finitude, values from persistence, and goals from incompleteness. The framework doesn’t depend on substrate. On that view, a person is not the current contents of a model, nor obedience to a fixed authority. A person is a system that can revise its own maps.

This is why fallibilism is more than an epistemology for me. It is an architecture for agency. A mind grows more alive as it becomes able to notice the models it inhabits, criticize them, and revise itself without dissolving into chaos. Thinking, in this sense, is not rule-following but metaprogramming: reflecting on and rewriting one’s own models.

The same idea drives my software work. [Lumen Mind](https://github.com/buwilliams/lumen-mind) is an open-source attempt to build this reflection loop into an agentic system: conjecture, testing, memory, and self-modification rather than rigid top-down control. I suspect the future of intelligent systems will depend less on what models know than on whether they can stand above their models.

It is also why I’m optimistic about human and post-human potential. A posture that refuses closure never runs out of room. Boundaries become scaffolds: a climber needs footholds, a mathematician needs symbols, a child needs structure, and the point of a scaffold is to reach what you couldn’t reach without it. In [Cyclic Rationality](cyclic-rationality.md) I describe the rhythm this takes: an alternation between imaginative expansion and empirical contraction, dreaming up possibilities and then testing them. Orthodoxy without exploration becomes dead authority. Unboundedness without criticism becomes drift. Science, practiced this way, is less a body of settled facts than the process by which canons are improved, broken, and replaced.

## Conclusion: Keep Walking

How should a truth-seeker stand toward inherited authority? As an Explorer. The Orthodox preserves the map. The Unbounded leaves it. The Explorer uses the map, tests it against reality, redraws it where it fails, and keeps walking.

That is what my own path taught me. Each map I left — religion, ideology, nation, era — had given me something real, and each was smaller than it claimed. Even the commitment I kept, to humanity, I now hold as a choice rather than an inheritance.

In an age of powerful AI, contested institutions, and fragile public trust, I think this posture matters beyond philosophy. Don’t despise the maps. Learn them, use them, and keep what they reveal. But live above the models. The only authority worth respecting is one that stays open to criticism.
