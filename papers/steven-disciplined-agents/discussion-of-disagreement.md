# Discussion of Disagreement

## Reliability as a matrix

If required reliability is a threshold, feasibility should depend on whether attainable reliability exceeds required reliability. A high-required, high-attainable task may still be unsafe when attainable reliability remains below the threshold. The four quadrants do not capture that relationship clearly.

Weighted averages can also hide fatal weaknesses. Strong classification or execution should not compensate for having no recovery path when a failure is catastrophic or irreversible. Some factors should operate as hard gates rather than as values that can be averaged away.

## States rather than rates

The paper acknowledges that both forms of reliability move, but its operating tool is still a snapshot. My essays emphasize rates, moving boundaries, expiring models, and the danger of treating a current task category as permanent. Evidence age and the rate and direction of movement on both axes may matter as much as present placement.

## Customer willingness is not the same as value or legitimacy

Observed willingness to delegate does not prove that an offering serves the customer's interests, preserves autonomy, handles externalities, or assigns accountability properly. Customer behavior is useful evidence, but it is not a complete moral or strategic test.

The framework would also benefit from explicit conditions that could refute it. Several anecdotes support the proposal, but claims such as "the matrix predicts" and "late capital cannot repair the loss" are stronger than the evidence presented.

## Core disagreement

My deeper disagreement concerns what the framework is optimizing. The paper treats current customer willingness as a gate. I see that as a form of status quo bias.

Customers do not arrive with fixed expectations. Their expectations change with technology and with the possibilities businesses place in front of them. Step-change products often succeed because they offer something customers could not clearly request or evaluate in advance. If innovators had treated existing comfort and trust as hard boundaries, many transformative technologies would never have been attempted.

Technological progress has always involved losses, failures, and some suffering. That reality does not excuse reckless experimentation, but preventing every failure is not the right objective. The better question is how to make failure survivable while continuing to explore.

That means limiting blast radius, preserving reversibility, compensating people who are harmed, protecting vulnerable populations, providing recovery paths, and preventing catastrophic or irreversible outcomes. Small and bounded experiments remain useful, but they should be launchpads for ambitious learning rather than merely tests of what customers already tolerate.

The alternative principle is:

> Explore the frontier boldly, accept that learning has costs, and design systems to prevent or mitigate catastrophic and irreversible harm rather than trying to prevent every failure.

Customer trust then becomes an evolving result of exploration, not its starting boundary. Reliability supports exploration rather than governing its horizon. The proper constraint is survivable failure, not conformity to current customer expectations.
