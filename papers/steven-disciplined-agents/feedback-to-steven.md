# Feedback to Steven

Steven,

I found a great deal to agree with in the paper. I especially like the distinction between model capability and dependable workflow performance, the emphasis on learning from real use, and the insistence that recovery paths must be designed alongside automation. The observation that AI can compress execution faster than organizations can learn also strongly matches what I have seen. Narrow deployments, human escalation, reversibility, and explicit mitigation are all valuable tools.

My main disagreement is with making current customer willingness to delegate the governing constraint. I think that introduces a strong status quo bias. Customers do not merely reveal fixed expectations; their expectations change with the technology and with the possibilities businesses place in front of them. Step-change products often delight or surprise customers precisely because they offer something customers could not have clearly requested in advance.

More broadly, technological progress has always involved losses, failures, and some degree of suffering. That does not excuse reckless experimentation, but I do not think eliminating failure—or remaining within existing trust boundaries—is the right objective. If humanity had treated present comfort and willingness as hard gates, many transformative technologies would never have been explored.

I would prefer a framework that exhorts firms to explore the frontier boldly while mitigating the worst consequences. The central question would become: How can we make failures survivable? That means limiting blast radius, preserving reversibility, compensating people who are harmed, protecting vulnerable populations, providing recovery paths, and preventing catastrophic or irreversible outcomes.

Under that framing, customer trust is an evolving result of exploration rather than its starting boundary. Small and bounded experiments remain important, but they are launchpads for ambitious learning—not merely tests of what customers already tolerate.

So my disagreement is not that reliability is unimportant. It is that reliability should support exploration rather than govern its horizon. I would frame the constraint as survivable failure, not conformity to current customer expectations. Fortune favors the bold, provided the bold take responsibility for mitigating the harms created along the way.
